# Finding the idea

The picture is the poster. There's almost no type, so the whole job of being seen falls on one image, and the
image is only as good as the idea behind it. The NVIDIA team made about 25 well-crafted versions before one worked;
every reject was beautiful and the wrong idea. Spend your effort here.

## 1. Collect the subject's world

The subject is the company, brand, product or project the poster is for. Before any idea, write these into
`brief.md`, from real sources (its own site, an IR page for a listed company, Wikipedia, recent news). Facts only;
mark anything unsure.

- **The name:** what it literally means or sounds like (BlackRock, Dollar Tree, Robinhood, Caterpillar, Apple).
- **The logo:** what it depicts (Ferrari's horse, Meta's infinity, Apple's apple, Shell's scallop, Gemini's
  sparkle) and the brand colours, as hex values from the company's brand page or its logo file.
- **The founder or CEO:** a signature object, garment, habit or phrase the public knows (Jensen's leather jacket,
  Karp's hair). Objects, never faces.
- **The product:** the one everybody pictures (the Big Mac, the 911, the Birkin), and what it's made of.
- **The words:** mottos, slogans and lines people repeat ("Move fast and break things", "Because you're worth it",
  "So like how long you been doing Uber?").
- **The material:** what the business physically handles (price tags, boxes, .com names, cockroaches, ore, cards).
- **The heritage:** how it started (Hermès made harnesses; Nintendo printed playing cards for 75 years before games).
- **Its story right now:** what people are talking about this month: a launch, a record, a succession, a price
  change, a rivalry. On an earnings poster, the question investors are asking this quarter. Search the news from the
  last four weeks.
- **The culture around it:** films, memes, songs and shared experiences tied to the company.

## 2. Write at least 20 ideas

One line each: the picture, then the move in brackets. Cover at least six of the moves below. Write fast and don't
judge yet; the good idea is usually past the first ten, because the first ten are what everyone thinks of.

| Move | What it does | From the swipe file |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The name, made literal | Picture the words of the name, then take a second step | BlackRock's pixel rock, Dollar Tree's tree of price tags, Robinhood's fox archer |
| The logo, made real | Turn the mark into a real thing, or a real thing into the mark | Ferrari's black horse, Apple's circuit apple, Meta's infinity goggles, Gemini's crown |
| The founder's signature | The object that stands for the person | Jensen's jacket on a coat rack, Karp's hair |
| The product as fine art | Shoot the everyday product like a luxury campaign, or repeat it into a shape | McDonald's falling burgers, Porsche's curve of 911s, Dole's banana plant at night |
| The thing they own | Repeat the asset until it's a texture | Verisign's wall of .com |
| The thing they fight | The unglamorous problem the business solves, made beautiful | Rollins' cockroaches |
| The words they're known for | Make the motto or the cliché do what it says | Meta's shattering motto, Palantir's "software that dominates", Uber's small talk |
| The heritage | Where it began, in a medium from that time | Hermès' painted carriage, Caterpillar's archival collage |
| The medium is the message | Use the product, or an object from its history, as the poster itself | Figma's canvas, a Post-it note for 3M, a ticket stub for Live Nation |
| Its story right now | One image that asks the question everyone is asking | NVIDIA's empty jacket: leaving or coming back? |
| The brand colour as the picture | The colour system alone, as a pattern | Nu's purple pixels |

### Every idea needs a second step

The first step finds the company's thing (the name, the founder's object, the product); the second step makes it say
something. The posters people share all take both:

- Dollar Tree's tree is the name; that its leaves are $1.25, $1.50 and $3.00 tags (the price that stopped being a
  dollar) is the second step.
- BlackRock's rock is the name; drawing a $15-trillion firm as a child's 8-bit mountain is the second step.
- NVIDIA's jacket is the founder; taking him out of it, so it asks "leaving or coming back?", is the second step.
- Ferrari's horse is the logo; making it a real animal rearing in red shadow is the second step.

A first step alone (a cruise ship with a Ferris wheel for a company called Carnival; the product shot gorgeously in its
natural habitat) is a pun or a picture, not an idea, and critics call it "only a pun". When an idea is stuck there,
don't re-render it: look for its second step (a material, a number, a medium, a tension with its news), or move to
the next idea.

## 3. Score them and keep three

Score each idea 1 to 5 on six questions. Write the scores down.

1. **Instant.** Would a stranger scrolling X get it in three seconds, with the logo covered? A picture that needs the
   caption to make sense scores 1. Check the reverse too: if the picture looks like another company's logo or mascot
   (a lone green feather is Robinhood's), strangers will name the wrong company.
2. **Only them.** Swap in a competitor's logo. If the poster still works (a rocket for any growth stock, a chip for
   any chipmaker, a burger for any burger chain), it scores 1. Dollar Tree's tags carry Dollar Tree's own prices;
   that's a 5.
3. **Surprise.** Is it the first thing anyone would picture? The obvious literal image scores 2 at most. Surprise
   usually comes from a second step: not "a can of soup" for Campbell's but one red-and-white can on a gallery plinth
   under a museum spotlight, because Warhol already made it art.
4. **Story.** Is there a wink, a tension or a question? The empty jacket asks "leaving or coming back?"; cockroaches on
   a pest-control poster are a dare. A plain product shot scores 1. Test it with the caption: write the reply a fan
   would post under it, in twelve words or fewer ("is the hot dog still $1.50?", "he's not coming back, is he").
   If you can't write one that a stranger would get, Story is 2 at most, however beautiful the picture.
5. **Makeable.** Can it be made to look real with the tools here (an image model, code, real photos, collage)? Ideas
   that need a real person's face, legible generated text, or a crowd of small figures score 1, because those are
   where pictures look fake.
6. **Beautiful.** Without the info, would someone hang it on a wall or use it as an album cover?

Keep the three with the highest lowest score (an idea with a 1 anywhere is out, however high the rest). Break ties
toward the one with fewer elements. Write the three as one-line pitches in `brief.md`.

## 4. Pick one, then subtract

Picture each of the three as a finished poster at phone size, next to the refs sheet. Pick the one that would stop
your thumb. Then take things away: every object, colour and effect that the idea survives without goes. The NVIDIA
team removed Jensen himself and kept the jacket. You're done when removing anything else would break the idea.

## Ideas that always lose

- **Finance clip art:** bulls and bears, rockets and arrows for growth, charts, coins and dollar signs, piggy banks,
  handshakes, globes, light bulbs, chess pieces, puzzle pieces, gears, a ticker tape, the Wall Street sign. Unless the
  user asked for an earnings poster, keep money out of the idea altogether: the subject's world is its product and
  its people, not its share price.
- **The logo made of something** ("the NVIDIA logo made of chips"). It's a template, and an image model can't draw the
  logo anyway.
- **The headquarters, the CEO's press photo, or the product on white.** That's the press kit, not a poster.
- **A meme or a screenshot joke.** The eBay listing was "a bit funny but not it".
- **An effect looking for an idea:** spirals, glitches, pixel storms, blur. If the effect is the most interesting
  thing in the brief, there's no idea yet.
- **A beautiful picture of the company's world with no wink:** the product in its natural habitat, lit gorgeously,
  saying nothing (the Dole poster gets away with it once; a series can't). It fails the caption test. Keep it as the
  picture and find the twist that makes it say something, or move to the next idea.
- **A wink only insiders get.** A reference to this week's executive memo or a rumour three people follow is a
  private joke. The best ideas work on two levels: anyone gets the picture, insiders get the second meaning.
- **A wink that needs explaining.** An empty slot, a missing piece or a tiny odd detail meant as a joke reads as a
  mistake to everyone who doesn't get it, and critics call it a generated slip. If the wink isn't readable at phone
  size, it isn't there; make it the picture or drop it.
- **Two ideas at once.** A collage of five references to the company reads as a mood board.
- **Mockery.** Layoffs, deaths, accidents, lawsuits and customers' bad days are never the joke. The wink has to be one
  the company's own fans would share.

## When the company's world is thin

Some companies have no famous founder, logo story or product image (a chip-equipment maker, a utility, an insurer).
Go to the material and the words: what does the business physically touch, move or store, and what single number or
phrase defines it? Verisign had nothing but .com; the poster is nothing but .com. Rollins had cockroaches.
